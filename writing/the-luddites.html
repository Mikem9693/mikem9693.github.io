The Luddites: A Tragedy of Misunderstanding

A protest that shook the world became the go-to name for those we consider behind the times.

We’re all guilty of it: a colleague challenges a new solution, your parents question your app idea or someone pokes a little too far into your vibe coding. They’re a Luddite, scared of change, and their points are roundly dismissed by uttering that one name.

It’s a far cry from the realities of the Luddites, the skilled workers pushed beyond breaking point. Economic and political victims are now shorthand for those who question before progressing.

That background is the context many are missing when throwing around the name of those who came before them and paved the way for the standards which followed. A long-running war, rising costs and politicians seemingly unbothered or unable to react to it all. Those are the conditions that led the skilled artisans of Nottingham, Yorkshire and Lancashire to turn to violence between 1811 and 1816.

Their rage was not against the machines; it was the reaction of helplessness. Skilled workers who had worked with machinery all their lives were suddenly being replaced by machines, losing their wages and seeing the world become unaffordable.

A short outline of the challenges Luddites faced:

  • War and trade blockades
  • Soaring food prices
  • Ignored by Parliament
  • Cheaper labour and cheaper goods
  • Falling pay and rising frame rents
  • No right to organise or join a union
  • Existing protections disappearing
  • No right to vote

How a protest became an insult

Many understand that the Luddites lost in their fight; the evidence to many is the laptop I’m writing this essay on. The progress kept coming and delivered us to a place where even our own thinking can be outsourced, if we so choose.

The much more tragic loss comes later: the loss of their name, their fight and their struggle. They were labelled as ignorant wreckers by most of the press; the tone was set early and has survived until today. By the time the turn of the century arrived, it was a common expression, and the fight a century before had long been forgotten.

Turn a word into an insult and people stop asking what it meant.

The importance of this should be more and more obvious as the parallels mount up. The Luddites were not afraid of change or machines. They challenged who should decide how new technology is used, who carries the cost and, above all else, who benefits. The same arguments are brewing as “AI Brain Fry” has been observed by US BCG in 2026, with 14% agreeing that they are suffering from mental exhaustion brought on by the changing ways of working as AI becomes more and more embedded.

The parallels in plain sight

  • The squeeze comes before the machine. In 1811, war and failed harvests had already pushed workers to the edge. In 2026 the UK jobs market is flat, vacancies are falling and youth unemployment reached 16.4% this summer, an 11-year high.
  • The first rung goes first. The Luddites fought “colts”, untrained workers used to bypass the apprenticeship system. Today AI is taking over the routine tasks that beginners used to learn on, and there are now 141 applications for every graduate job, up from 86 in 2023.
  • The machine takes the blame. The frames absorbed the anger for hardship that war and harvests had largely caused. Now, economists argue over how much of the hiring slump is AI and how much is rising employment costs and a weak economy.

I’ll be the first to admit the parallel isn’t exact. We enjoy rights the working class of the 19th century were denied, which gives us a chance to bring about change without looking to a “General Ludd” character. Those rights are already being used; Hollywood writers won limits on how studios can use AI, but it’s fair to ask how much influence a graduate trying to break into a career really has over a world moving in a direction that makes them increasingly redundant.

The Luddites paid a huge price. Seventeen men were hanged at York in 1813, and many more were transported. Not even the most committed AI sceptic is at that point yet. But neither were the Luddites, until war, rising prices and an indifferent Parliament left them nowhere else to go.

Before you reach for the word

The next time you reach for “Luddite”, it’s worth a moment’s reflection on the point the person is actually making, and what their concerns really are. The rage is very rarely against the machine. The machine is just the closest and easiest thing to hit.
